# Traditional Yogurt

**Category:** thermophilic fermented milk\
**Historical region:** West and Central Asia, Anatolia and the Balkans; many local traditions\
**Defining culture:** symbiotic *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* under Codex\
**Evidence status:** standard-backed identity; household history and individual lineages vary

## Identity, history and production

Yogurt has deep regional traditions, but a broad historical name should not imply one preserved culture or precise place of invention. For the internationally standardized product, Codex specifies the two living starter species above. Other microorganisms may be added, but their presence does not replace the defining pair. [GEN001]

Traditional backslopping transfers a previous batch; modern manufacture heats and standardizes milk, inoculates selected cultures and rapidly cools near a chosen acidity. Cow, sheep, goat and buffalo milk traditions exist, while composition and texture vary with protein, fat and heat treatment.

## Fermentation, sensory and acidification

Controlled yogurt commonly ferments in the low-to-mid 40s °C until about pH 4.5–4.6. The catalog retains **40–45 °C / 3–8 h** as a practical envelope, not a standard-mandated rule. A primary Greek isolate study demonstrates wide differences in acidification, viscosity and aroma across 74 *S. thermophilus* and 75 *L. bulgaricus* isolates. Species identity alone cannot predict process time. [YOG001]

Set yogurt is incubated in the final container; stirred yogurt is broken after fermentation. Sourness, acetaldehyde aroma, syneresis and gel strength depend on strain pairing and milk treatment. Cooling slows but does not always stop post-acidification.

## Strain and EPS evidence

The Greek study reports isolate populations but the abstract does not supply stable accession codes for individual cultures; none are invented here. Strain pairing matters because proteolysis and metabolite exchange support mutual growth. Some strains produce EPS and increase viscosity, while others do not. “*S. thermophilus*” is therefore species-level evidence, not a strain specification.

Yeasts and molds are not defining yogurt organisms. Their detection in household samples may represent adjunct ecology or contamination and needs product-specific evidence.

## Propagation, storage and distinctions

Backslopping can sustain household yogurt but may change strain proportions. Industrial mother cultures and direct-vat starters offer different control. No universal number of transfers or shelf life is assigned; refrigerated stability requires product-specific viability, pH, contamination and sensory measurements.

Traditional Yogurt differs from Bulgarian Yogurt by breadth of provenance and from Greek Yogurt because straining is a concentration step. Acidophilus, AB and ABT milks use other defining formulations. Evidence is strong for Codex identity and strain-dependent performance, but heterogeneous for household temperatures, fungal ecology and long-term transfer stability.
