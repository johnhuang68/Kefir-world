# Greek Yogurt

**Category:** concentrated fermented milk / strained yogurt\
**Traditional region:** Greece and eastern Mediterranean strained-milk traditions\
**Starter ecology:** usually the classical yogurt pair; concentration does not define a new culture\
**Evidence status:** standard-backed product class; manufacturing method must be specified

## Identity and production

Codex places *stragisto* (strained yogurt), Labneh, Ymer and Ylette within “Concentrated Fermented Milk,” whose protein is increased before or after fermentation. Thus Greek Yogurt is primarily a composition and processing style. It does not require a unique “Greek strain.” [GEN001]

Traditional production ferments milk as yogurt and drains whey through cloth. Modern dairies may centrifuge yogurt or increase protein before fermentation by ultrafiltration or milk ingredients. These routes can produce similar composition while differing in whey stream, texture and microbial stress.

## Fermentation, milk and sensory properties

The base yogurt commonly uses *S. thermophilus* with *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*. The catalog retains the base process envelope **40–45 °C / 3–8 h**, explicitly separate from refrigerated draining. Cow, sheep and goat milks are documented regional matrices; industrial markets commonly use cow milk.

Concentration raises protein and total solids, creating a dense, creamy body and reducing free whey in the finished product. Fat level is independent: both nonfat and full-fat products exist. Straining does not erase post-acidification or guarantee low syneresis.

## Microbial and strain evidence

Traditional Greek yogurts have yielded many isolates of the two classical species with different acidification, viscosity and aroma behavior. The 2001 study examined 74 streptococcal and 75 lactobacillus isolates, but its abstract does not justify assigning any named strain code to the entire category. [YOG001]

EPS is strain-dependent and can influence mouthfeel before and after concentration. A dense final product is not EPS evidence because protein concentration itself raises viscosity. No required yeast or mold species defines Greek Yogurt.

## Propagation, storage and distinctions

Household yogurt may be backslopped before draining; industrial products use selected cultures. Finished strained yogurt is a poor proxy for starter transfer because drainage and storage alter the matrix. Shelf life depends on cooling, package, hygiene and live-culture specifications; no universal duration is assigned.

Greek Yogurt differs from [Labneh](labneh.md) mainly by regional/product conventions, salt and degree of concentration rather than a necessary microbial boundary. It differs from unstrained Traditional Yogurt through solids concentration. Evidence is strong for the Codex class and Greek isolate diversity, weaker for one universal traditional process and strain lineage.
