# Dahi

**Category:** South Asian acid-coagulated fermented milk / curd\
**Traditional region:** Indian subcontinent; regionally distinct Himalayan traditions\
**Starter ecology:** household backslopped communities or selected mesophilic/thermophilic cultures\
**Evidence status:** literature-backed profile; substantial regional and starter-specific variation

## Origin, history and scope

Dahi has a long history in South Asian diets. FAO records references in ancient Hindu texts but explicitly says its precise origin is unknown; a single invention date or birthplace is therefore not assigned. Cow milk, buffalo milk and their mixtures are established substrates. FAO describes a glossy, cohesive curd with a clean acidic flavour; cow-milk versions tend toward yellow-white and buffalo-milk versions toward cream-white. [DAH001]

Here **curd means the South Asian fermented-milk usage**, not every coagulated cheese curd. Dahi/thayir encompasses diverse household practices: a Kerala study of 200 households found regional differences in inoculation, containers and incubation. This category should not be reduced to one universal bacterial formula. [DAH015]

This profile primarily covers plain dahi. Sweet concentrated **Mishti Doi/Misti Dahi** has its own dataset entry; process and culture findings from it are not automatically transferred to plain dahi. The separately listed Indonesian **Dadih/Dadiah** is also a distinct product, despite its similar spelling.

## Fermentation window

| Context | Reported temperature and time | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional household description | Milk cooled after boiling; then **overnight**, without a universal measured temperature | FAO describes previous-day dahi/buttermilk at **0.5–1%** and insulation in colder areas. [DAH001] |
| Mesophilic process summary | **22 °C; 12–14 h** | Review table groups dahi and related Indian cultured products; an illustrative process, not a household survey or universal optimum. [DAH003] |
| Warmer process summary | **37 °C; 5–8 h** | Alternative culture-dependent process in the same review. [DAH003] |
| Defined single-strain trial | **37 °C; 7 h**, reaching **pH 4.2** | *Lactococcus lactis* W8 in cow milk; direct experimental result. [DAH005] |
| Bhutanese traditional dahi | **Room temperature; 2–3 days** | Regional description; no numerical room-temperature range supplied. Do not reinterpret as a recommended long warm incubation. [DAH009] |
| Buffalo-milk process experiment | **20.8–29.2 °C** | Experimental design range, not a demonstrated optimum for all dahi. [DAH004] |
| Modern instructional endpoint | **37 °C**, until **pH 4.4–4.5**, then rapid cooling below **5 °C** | Endpoint-controlled example; a fixed fermentation duration is not specified. [DAH002] |

For comparative plotting, **22–37 °C with 5–14 h** can represent the two cited controlled-production examples, provided the annotation preserves the temperature/time pairs. It excludes the colder/longer Himalayan traditions and should not imply that every temperature in the interval works with every starter. A map of traditional household fermentation needs accession-specific records rather than this simplified band.

## Traditional and modern production

### Household production

The established sequence is heating milk, cooling it, mixing in a previous batch and leaving it undisturbed until set. The retained product transfers the community to the next batch; there are no kefir grains to recover. Some historically recorded processes begin without an added starter. These descriptions document practice rather than a standardized validation of safety. [DAH001]

The Kerala household survey links climate and preparation differences with microbiological quality, including contamination concerns. Its findings support recording actual incubation temperature, starter quantity and vessel choice instead of treating “room temperature” as a reproducible parameter. [DAH015]

### Controlled production

An Indian university teaching protocol uses standardized, homogenized milk, heated at **85–90 °C for 15–30 min**, cooled to **37 °C**, and inoculated with **1–1.5%** selected dahi culture. Cup fermentation is followed by rapid cooling at the specified endpoint and storage at **4–5 °C**. This is one manufacturing example; starter-supplier requirements may differ. [DAH002]

Defined dahi cultures can also be preserved in bulk freeze-dried form. A controlled cow-/buffalo-milk comparison tested a lactococcal mixture, with and without *Lactobacillus acidophilus* R and *Bifidobacterium bifidum* I adjuncts. Those are experimental formulations rather than the microbiome of all commercial or traditional dahi. [DAH012]

## Milk, sensory properties and texture

- **Cow and buffalo milk:** conventional substrates; mixed milk is also documented. [DAH001]
- **Goat milk:** included among dahi substrates in the dairy-technology review, but should remain separately specified when comparing texture. [DAH003]
- **Himalayan milk systems:** the Sikkim/Arunachal study includes cow- and yak-milk fermented products. Its pooled findings do not establish the milk origin of every dahi sample. [DAH008]
- **Fat and solids:** whole, reduced-fat and fat-free experimental formulations require separate records; equal starter composition does not ensure equal gel structure.

Dahi can be spoonable or served as a stirred beverage. A cohesive acid gel, moderate sourness and, in some cultures, a buttery aroma are relevant descriptors. Diacetyl-producing lactococci and *Leuconostoc* contribute to the aromatic styles. [DAH003]

Texture depends on milk composition, acidification rate, heating and microbial polysaccharides. In the buffalo-milk modelling study, temperature, total solids and inoculum jointly affected the gel. Greater incubation temperature increased syneresis; conditions giving a firmer gel lay around **27–29.2 °C**, **14.5–15% total solids**, and **2.5% inoculum** within that experiment. These values describe that design, not a universal best recipe. [DAH004]

## Microbial ecology: species-level evidence

| Taxon or group | Evidence and product context | What is not established |
|---|---|---|
| *Lactococcus lactis*; historical *Lc. lactis* subsp. *cremoris*; *Lc. lactis* biovar *diacetylactis* | Defined mesophilic dahi blend tested in cow and buffalo milk. [DAH012] | Presence of those particular strains in a household starter. |
| *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* | Especially prominent in the dahi component of the Himalayan amplicon survey; *Lc. lactis* was also prominent. [DAH008] | Species identification from short 16S reads is not strain identification or proof of EPS production. |
| *Streptococcus* and *Lactobacillus* sensu lato | Dominant LAB genera in three northern Indian regional dahi samples/profiles; genus-level findings are retained here. [DAH007] | The study's profile is not representative of all Indian dahi; 16S abundance alone does not establish thermophilic growth performance. |
| *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* | Used in the industrial dahi description in FAO; named *L. bulgaricus* historically. [DAH001] | An obligatory two-species composition in traditional dahi. |
| *Enterococcus faecium* | Dahi-derived Bhutanese isolate DH4:05, identified using partial 16S sequencing. [DAH009] | Automatic probiotic status or suitability as a food starter. |
| *Lactobacillus acidophilus*; *Bifidobacterium bifidum* | Intentionally added to specific experimental dahi cultures. [DAH012] | Natural prevalence in traditional dahi. |
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Kluyveromyces marxianus*, *K. lactis* | Dahi yeast isolates reported in a Sikkim study of cow-milk products; identification used the LSU D1/D2 region. [DAH013] | Yeasts being required for all dahi or making it equivalent to kefir. |
| Other environmental bacteria | Northern Indian 16S profiles included non-LAB detections; the authors discuss possible production-environment contamination. [DAH007] | Intentional starter function, viability or beneficial activity. |

The Himalayan 16S study reports *Lactobacillus helveticus* and acetic acid bacteria across several fermented products. Aggregate percentages from chhurpi, churkam, dahi and butter are **not dahi-only abundance estimates**. The dataset therefore emphasizes taxa discussed specifically for dahi rather than copying its entire pooled inventory. [DAH008]

### Yeasts and filamentous fungi

Yeasts are documented members of some traditional dahi communities, with named isolates below. This does not make visible mould growth a defining quality attribute. The reviewed evidence does not establish an obligatory filamentous-fungal surface culture for dahi.

A Bangladeshi study examined bacterial and fungal communities in dahi, chanar-misti, paneer and borhani. Its accessible abstract reports several organisms specifically for **chanar-misti** and gives some counts across products; those findings are not reassigned to dahi. In particular, *Clavispora lusitaniae* is not added to this dahi inventory on that basis. [DAH016]

**W8 has genomic traceability:** whole-genome shotgun project **QYRO00000000**, version **QYRO01000000**, and raw reads **SRR7868294**. These are sequence accessions, not additional strain names. Genome-based predictions of function are distinguished from observed fermentation and from clinical evidence. [DAH006]

## EPS and slime-forming organisms

EPS production is strain-dependent and useful in texture design, especially when reducing milk fat. B-6 and KT-24 produced chemically different polysaccharides and improved viscosity and resistance to whey separation in fat-free dahi. Their effects should not be assigned to every *Lc. lactis* isolate. [DAH010]

In the V10 comparison, EPS-producing cultures increased viscosity, adhesiveness and stickiness while reducing gel firmness relative to the non-EPS comparator. Thus “thicker” and “firmer” are different measurements. Ropy texture is an optional culture trait, not the defining identity of dahi. [DAH011]

## Acidification behaviour

Acidification is a process trajectory, not simply a terminal pH. In the buffalo-milk experiment, lactose consumption followed first-order kinetics under the tested conditions, while inoculum, solids and incubation temperature affected acid development and physical texture. [DAH004]

W8 reached pH 4.2 after 7 h at 37 °C in the original production paper. Its later genome announcement summarizes the result as within 6 h; this profile retains the **original experimental paper's 7 h** rather than merging the two into a falsely precise general range. [DAH005], [DAH006]

Published endpoints answer different questions: the industrial teaching protocol targets pH 4.4–4.5, whereas the sampled Bhutanese dahi averaged pH **3.7 ± 0.17**. The latter is an observation of sampled products, not a manufacturing target. [DAH002], [DAH009]

Measure pH and titratable acidity separately: they are not interchangeable. For accession work, record milk solids, inoculum, temperature, time to visible setting and serial pH readings; these recommendations are the repository's synthesis of the process evidence.

## Propagation, storage and robustness

Traditional backslopping perpetuates a living community under the household's conditions. **Continued ability to set milk is not proof that community composition or strain ratios remain unchanged.** The regional variation observed in surveys is compatible with strong effects of process history, although those cross-sectional studies do not directly measure multigeneration stability. [DAH007], [DAH015]

Bulk freeze-dried selected starters performed in both cow and buffalo milk in a controlled comparison. That finding supports culture preservation for the tested formulations, not indefinite preservation of an uncharacterized household community. [DAH012]

At **7 °C**, an experimental low-fat dahi with lactococci and *L. acidophilus/L. casei* adjuncts showed changing viable counts, declining pH and increasing proteolysis during storage. The panel noted bitterness and dislike at day 8. The abstract also concludes acceptability up to 8 days, an internally awkward boundary; this profile does **not** translate it into a guaranteed eight-day shelf life. [DAH014]

W8 dahi showed inhibition of an experimentally introduced *Listeria monocytogenes* strain during refrigerated challenge testing. This is evidence for a particular protective starter under tested conditions, not proof that fermentation eliminates all hazards or that an uncharacterized dahi contains nisin. [DAH005]

Robustness records should separate starter revival, repeat-batch acidification, retention of EPS/aroma traits, finished-product sensory shelf life and microbiological safety. No universal number of successful transfers, freeze/thaw cycles or refrigerator days is established by the sources assembled here.

## Distinctions from similar products

- **Yogurt/Bulgarian yogurt:** some dahi formulations use the same two bacterial species, but the dahi category also includes mesophilic lactococcal and diverse backslopped cultures. [DAH005]
- **Mishti Doi:** concentrated sweetened dahi with a distinct sensory/process specification; keep a separate formulation record. [DAH003]
- **Lassi:** a stirred/diluted dahi-derived beverage; its added ingredients and processing change the final product. [DAH002]
- **Datshi:** Bhutanese cheese-like downstream product. Its CH-prefixed isolates are not dahi isolates. [DAH009]
- **Kefir and ropy Nordic milks:** EPS or yeast detection alone does not establish shared starter ancestry, a grain ecosystem or interchangeable propagation requirements.

## Evidence quality and open questions

1. **Strongest for a specific process:** primary controlled trials of W8, EPS cultures and buffalo-milk acidification. Their findings transfer only with the relevant formulation and conditions.
2. **Ecological evidence:** culture isolation establishes recoverable organisms; marker-gene profiles describe detected DNA and relative composition. Neither provides an exhaustive inventory or clinical evidence.
3. **Sampling scope:** northern Indian, Himalayan, Kerala and Bangladeshi studies are regional datasets, not a census of South Asian dahi.
4. **Taxonomy:** historical organism names are preserved beside modern usage where helpful. Subspecies, biovars, isolate codes and sequence accessions are different levels of identification.
5. **Probiotic language:** tolerance to acid/bile and other laboratory screening are candidate traits; they do not establish a human health benefit or authorize an isolate as a starter.
6. **Historical/teaching sources:** FAO's 1990 account is valuable for traditional practice; older legal statements, market estimates and shelf-life claims are not treated as current regulations or universal facts.
7. **Remaining gaps:** standardized multi-season sampling, fungal community studies beyond selected isolates, strain-resolved tracking through backslopping, and comparable preservation trials for household cultures.

## Data-quality rule

Ranges describe cited production examples, not biological optima, food-safety guarantees or validated household instructions. Species detections, named isolates, deliberately added adjuncts and experimental protective cultures are kept separate. Unverified strain IDs and pooled-product abundance estimates must not enter a dahi accession record.
