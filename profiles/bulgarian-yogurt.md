# Bulgarian Yogurt

**Category:** thermophilic, predominantly lactic fermented milk; traditionally set yogurt\
**Traditional region:** Bulgaria, within the wider Balkan fermented-milk tradition\
**Alternate names:** Bulgarian yoghurt; kiselo mlyako; Българско кисело мляко\
**Starter ecology:** *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* + *Streptococcus thermophilus*, with additional taxa in some household samples\
**Evidence status:** literature-backed profile; category definitions, individual strains and process examples have different scopes

## Identity, origin and history

Bulgarian yogurt is a regional yogurt tradition, not proof that all yogurt originated in one country. Historical accounts connect its scientific description with Stamen Grigorov's work in 1905; claims about a precise ancient invention date remain less secure than the modern microbiology. [BUL001]

The EU registered **Българско кисело мляко / Bulgarsko kiselo mlyako** as a protected designation of origin (PDO) in 2023. That protected name has geographical and production requirements; a yogurt made elsewhere with the same two bacterial species is not thereby the PDO product. [BUL002], [BUL003]

Codex defines yogurt through the symbiotic use of *S. thermophilus* and *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*. This is a **species/subspecies definition**, not a requirement for a particular pair of strain numbers. Yeasts and molds are not defining starter organisms. [GEN001]

## Traditional and modern production

Households transfer a little previous yogurt into heated milk after cooling to an appropriate inoculation temperature. The PDO application describes this continuing backslopping tradition and the later development of selected industrial starter combinations. Cow, sheep, buffalo and goat milk, including mixtures, are documented in its product description. [BUL002]

A primary study investigated a separate traditional starter-initiation practice using plants in boiled sheep milk at about **45 °C**. It recovered yogurt bacteria from a minority of collected plants and tested their milk-fermentation performance. This supports one possible environmental source; it does not establish a universal historical origin or a modern starter-preparation recommendation. [BUL004]

Modern production uses selected starter cultures, controlled milk treatment and incubation, and cooling. In set production the inoculated milk coagulates in its final package; stirring breaks the gel and creates a different physical presentation. Milk protein concentration, fat and heat treatment also affect texture, independently of bacterial identity. [BUL001], [BUL010]

## Fermentation window

| Context | Temperature | Time / inoculum | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Published Bulgarian production description | **42–45 °C** | **2.5–4 h**; starter **0.5–2%** | Review-supported working window, not a surveyed household minimum/maximum [BUL001] |
| Plant-associated traditional starter initiation | About **45 °C** | Until coagulation; no universal time supplied | Primary-study context, not the catalog's standard production protocol [BUL004] |
| Household backslopping | Warm incubation, dependent on starter and practice | No single evidence-backed time covers all households | Keep separate from the controlled production window [BUL002] |
| Acid-gel formation | Endpoint governed by acidification | Casein's approximate isoelectric pH is **4.6** | General yogurt mechanism; not a uniquely Bulgarian release specification [BUL010] |

The catalog retains 42–45 °C and 2.5–4 h **with that production-description scope**. Culture growth temperatures on laboratory agar must not be substituted for milk-incubation conditions. No single inoculum ratio is prescribed across all Bulgarian starters.

## Sensory and texture characteristics

The PDO description emphasizes fresh lactic aroma, pronounced sourness and a uniform creamy body. The expected product is an acidified milk gel; milk source influences its character. These descriptive terms should not be converted into universal numerical viscosity or acidity values. [BUL002]

Acetaldehyde is a major yogurt aroma compound; other carbonyl compounds contribute. Acid-driven casein aggregation forms the main gel. Some starter strains produce extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), modifying water retention and flow without changing the product into a Nordic ropy milk. Milk solids and processing must be controlled before attributing a thick texture to EPS alone. [BUL010]

## Microbial ecology: species-level evidence

| Organism / group | Reported role and evidence | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* | Defining yogurt bacterium; proteolysis, acidification and strain-dependent EPS [GEN001], [BUL006] | Subspecies name is not a strain ID |
| *S. thermophilus* | Defining partner; acidification and metabolic cooperation [GEN001], [BUL007] | Its abundance and technological behavior depend on the pairing |
| *Lacticaseibacillus paracasei*, *L. rhamnosus*, *Lactobacillus helveticus*, *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* | Accompanying LAB reported in a four-household-yogurt sequencing study [BUL008] | Not mandatory starter constituents or a prevalence survey of all Bulgaria |
| *Lactococcus lactis*, *Pediococcus acidilactici*, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, *L. pseudomesenteroides* | Additional LAB in that study [BUL008] | Detection does not establish that each taxon drives fermentation |
| Yeasts and filamentous fungi | Not required by the yogurt starter definition [GEN001] | No reproducible product-defining fungal species or fungal strain is assigned here; this is not evidence that every household sample is fungus-free |

In the four-sample study, the two defining yogurt bacteria together represented **68.4–99.6%** of the reported community, with markedly different ratios between samples. Sequencing proportions are not viable-cell counts. Its culture isolation and typing add evidence about selected organisms but do not convert the whole community into a defined starter. [BUL008]

## Published strain-level designations

These are **examples tied to publications**, not a shopping list or a universal Bulgarian yogurt formula. A named research isolate, a collection accession and a commercial starter mixture are distinct identifiers.

| Taxon and published designation | Provenance / context | Reliably supported finding |
|---|---|---|
| *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* **B5** | Component of Bulgarian starter **LBB.BY 5-12** [BUL005] | Used with *S. thermophilus* A; LBB.BY 5-12 names the mixture, not another strain |
| *S. thermophilus* **A** | Same starter [BUL005] | Distinguished from added strains using DNA probes |
| *S. thermophilus* **6V** | Added to that starter experimentally [BUL005] | EPS-producing partner; do not infer that strain A or every *S. thermophilus* is equally ropy |
| *S. thermophilus* **N1** | Alternative added strain in the same study [BUL005] | Fast-acidifying phenotype in this experimental context |
| *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* **2038** | Industrial Meiji strain reported as originally isolated from Bulgaria [BUL006] | Complete genome; GenBank **CP000156** is its sequence accession, not a second strain |
| *S. thermophilus* **1131** | Industrial yogurt partner of 2038 in a Japanese mechanistic study [BUL007] | Oxygen-consuming NADH oxidase contributes to effective mixed-culture fermentation; Bulgarian geographic isolation is not established by this paper |
| *S. thermophilus* **CBC-S77** | Reported from homemade Bulgarian dairy foods [BUL009] | Draft genome **JABWOT000000000**; the paper's broad dairy-food provenance should not be silently narrowed to a yogurt-only isolate |

The 2006 work found that the two streptococcal strains in each three-component starter remained at approximately similar population proportions in the studied starter/product. This demonstrates compatibility for those combinations and measurements, not indefinite stability through household transfers. [BUL005]

## EPS and slime formation

The **6V** experiment is direct evidence for an EPS-producing streptococcus in a Bulgarian starter formulation. [BUL005] Genome analysis of **2038** identifies an EPS gene cluster differing from those of comparison strains. A gene cluster supports biosynthetic potential; it is not, by itself, a measurement of the polymer yield or viscosity in every milk. [BUL006]

The appropriate record is therefore “EPS depends on strain and process.” Neither the word *bulgaricus* nor a thick set gel proves long-thread slime production. Kefiran should not be used as a generic name for yogurt EPS.

## Acidification and microbial cooperation

The two yogurt species can stimulate one another through nutrient exchange. Mixed-culture transcriptomics using **S. thermophilus CNRZ1066** and **L. bulgaricus ATCC BAA-365** investigated interactions involving amino-acid, nucleotide and EPS metabolism. These are **mechanistic comparator strains**, not evidence of their presence in a Bulgarian household culture. [BUL011]

In the **1131/2038** pairing, experiments with an NADH-oxidase-defective mutant showed that the streptococcus's oxygen consumption materially affects fermentation. Thus oxygen exposure, starter physiology and partner compatibility can change acidification even at the same incubation temperature. This result is specific to the tested system; no numerical oxygen optimum is assigned to all yogurt. [BUL007]

pH measures hydrogen-ion activity; titratable acidity also reflects milk buffering. They must be recorded separately. The gel-formation pH is not the complete time-to-endpoint curve, and two milks reaching the same pH need not have identical titratable acidity or texture. [BUL010]

## Propagation, storage and robustness

- **Propagation:** previous-batch yogurt is a documented household inoculum. Keep lineage, milk, inoculum quantity and fermentation endpoint with each accession. The reviewed evidence does not establish a fixed maximum number of successful backslops. [BUL002]
- **Culture maintenance:** the 2038 genome paper discusses industrial maintenance and genomic differences. Successful industrial preservation of a named culture does not prove that unrestricted household transfers preserve the same strain ratio or genotype. [BUL006]
- **Finished-product storage:** the Bulgarian review describes cooling and storage up to **21 days** for its production account. It is not a validated shelf-life guarantee for an arbitrary household batch; a matched package, temperature and microbiological study would be needed for that inference. [BUL001]
- **Acidity during storage:** cooling slows starter metabolism; continued acidification and whey separation depend on the culture and process. General yogurt technology supports this mechanism, but a matched Bulgarian lineage-specific cold-storage curve remains a useful gap. [BUL010]
- **Robustness limits:** the oxygen experiment demonstrates sensitivity to a controllable process variable. It does not establish resistance to every temperature excursion, phage, freezing or drying. No product-wide survival percentage is available from the sources used here. [BUL007]

| Product / label | Key distinction |
|---|---|
| Yogurt made outside Bulgaria | May have the same two species; that alone establishes neither Bulgarian provenance nor PDO status [GEN001], [BUL003] |
| Greek / strained yogurt | Straining and concentration describe processing; bacterial species alone cannot identify a strained product |
| Caspian Sea Yogurt | Japanese mesophilic lactococcal culture has a different ecology; see [its profile](caspian-sea-yogurt.md) |
| Matsoni / Matzoon | Regional Caucasian tradition can share thermophilic species but requires its own provenance and process record; see [Matsoni](matsoni.md) |
| Viili / Långfil | Mesophilic traditions with distinct ropiness ecology; see [Viili](viili.md) and [Långfil](langfil.md) |

## Evidence quality and open questions

1. **High confidence:** Codex identity; the names and contexts of B5, A, 6V, N1 and 2038; the existence of the 2023 PDO registration.
2. **Context-bound primary evidence:** the four household samples, strain compatibility experiments and oxygen mechanism. None is a complete census of traditional Bulgarian yogurt.
3. **Review-supported process:** 42–45 °C / 2.5–4 h is useful for the catalog, but not a validated envelope for every household lineage.
4. **Authority is claim-specific:** a PDO document establishes its specification and records historical claims. Its statement that the bacterium only reproduces in Bulgaria is not adopted as a biological rule; industrial and experimental cultivation elsewhere is documented in BUL006–BUL007.
5. **Remaining gaps:** matched traditional-batch pH curves, longitudinal backslopping, yeast/ITS surveys, phage susceptibility and freeze/dry recovery for named accessions. Health or longevity claims are not established by isolation, genome sequencing or in-vitro metabolite production.

## Freshly Fermented commercial-starter instructions

For the supplier's freeze-dried product, milk is boiled and cooled before incubation at **42 °C for about 8 h**; a first batch may take up to **18 h**, followed by at least two hours of refrigeration. Later batches use about **1 tablespoon per litre** from product younger than seven days. The page does not disclose strain identities or evidence of Bulgarian PDO provenance. Its first-activation timing is therefore kept separate from the review-supported 42–45 °C / 2.5–4 h controlled production range. [FF009]
