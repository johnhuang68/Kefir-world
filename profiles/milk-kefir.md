# Milk Kefir

**Category:** grain-based fermented milk\
**Traditional region:** Caucasus / Eurasia; exact historical provenance is not a microbiological variety\
**Starter ecology:** dynamic multispecies kefir-grain consortium\
**Evidence status:** literature-backed profile; product-, species- and strain-level evidence distinguished

## Origin and traditional versus modern production

Milk kefir is historically associated with the Caucasus and surrounding Eurasian dairying traditions. Geographic labels describe provenance; they do not define standardized microbial varieties. Traditional production transfers intact grains between batches of cow, goat, sheep or other mammalian milk. Modern products may instead use freeze-dried grains, grain-derived inoculum or a defined “kefir-style” culture. These systems should not share a culture-accession identity without evidence. [MK001], [MK002], [MK003]

## Fermentation window

| Parameter | Evidence-based working range |
|---|---|
| Traditional incubation temperature | **20–25 °C** |
| Traditional fermentation time | **18–24 h** |
| Common grain inoculum | roughly **2–10% w/v** in published production studies/reviews |
| Typical endpoint | acidified fermented milk; endpoint depends on inoculum, milk and temperature |

Traditional artisanal production is commonly described as milk inoculated with kefir grains and fermented for approximately 18–24 h at 20–25 °C. The grains are then separated and reused. Milk heating, inoculum, oxygen exposure and agitation differ between studies, so the pair is a common practice band rather than a safety recipe. [MK001], [MK002]

Temperature should not be treated as a single universal optimum. Kefir grains contain organisms with different temperature preferences, particularly lactic acid bacteria (LAB), acetic acid bacteria (AAB), and yeasts. Modern reviews therefore describe 20–25 °C as a practical traditional/common operating range rather than a biologically universal optimum.

## Temperature effects

Controlled repeated-batch experiments show that temperature materially changes fermentation behavior. In one study comparing 20 °C, ambient 19–26 °C, and 25 °C over repeated 24 h cycles, **25 °C produced the highest grain biomass growth and faster sugar consumption**, while 20 °C produced slower biomass growth and a higher final pH.

Another morphology/biomass study found that temperatures above 25 °C accelerated acidification; at 30–40 °C the medium became strongly acidic within 24 h and grain biomass performance was reduced. This is why a higher temperature is not automatically 'better' even when acidification becomes faster.

### Practical interpretation

- **20 °C:** slower fermentation; may support good culture stability depending on grain origin and process.
- **20–25 °C:** core traditional/common range.
- **25 °C:** frequently associated with faster lactose/sugar utilization and strong grain biomass growth in controlled studies.
- **>25–30 °C:** fermentation accelerates, but microbial balance, acidity and grain growth may shift; do not assume it is optimal for long-term grain maintenance.

## Microbial ecosystem

Milk kefir is fundamentally different from a defined two-strain yogurt starter. Natural kefir grains are a physical microbial consortium containing multiple bacterial and yeast taxa embedded in a polysaccharide/protein matrix.

Major ecological groups reported across natural grains include:

- **Lactic acid bacteria (LAB)**
- **Yeasts**
- **Acetic acid bacteria (AAB)**

Frequently reported taxa include *Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens*, *Lentilactobacillus kefiri*, *Lactococcus lactis*, *Leuconostoc* spp., and yeasts such as *Kluyveromyces marxianus*; composition varies substantially with grain origin, milk and cultivation history. [MK002], [MK003]

> Important: there is no single universal 'kefir microbiome'. Geographic origin, substrate, cultivation conditions and analytical method can produce substantially different profiles.

## Kefiran and grain structure

A defining extracellular polysaccharide associated with milk kefir grains is **kefiran**, a heteropolysaccharide composed principally of glucose and galactose. *Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens* is strongly associated with kefiran production in the literature.

Kefiran contributes to the grain matrix and can affect viscosity and viscoelastic properties of fermented milk systems. Grain biomass increase reflects both microbial growth and production of matrix material.

## Morphology

Natural milk kefir grains are generally irregular, lobed/cauliflower-like structures ranging from small granules to structures over centimeters in size. Morphology is not sufficient to identify geographic origin or microbiological composition.

## Grain origin matters

Published comparisons of grains from different geographical sources show differences in bacterial/yeast composition and volatile/aroma profiles. Therefore Kefir World treats each independently sourced grain as a **culture accession**, not merely another sample of a universal identical starter.

Future accession records should include:

- accession ID
- supplier/source
- claimed geographic origin
- acquisition date
- milk substrate
- temperature history
- inoculum ratio
- 24 h pH
- biomass growth rate
- aroma / acidity / carbonation
- grain morphology photographs
- sequencing or culture data when available

## Grain preservation warning

Freeze-drying/lyophilization can preserve kefir cultures, but published reviews report that dehydration/lyophilization may alter lactose metabolism and bacterial profiles relative to the original grains. A revived dried grain should therefore not automatically be assumed microbiologically identical to its pre-drying state.

## Strain-level evidence boundary

The cited reviews contain many named isolates, but a species name, a reference genome and a strain tested outside the sampled grain are not interchangeable. This profile therefore does not declare one “universal kefir strain.” Future strain records require a source grain, isolation method, actual designation and, ideally, a public accession. Detection in fermented milk also does not establish a health effect or permanent intestinal colonization.

## Data-quality rule

Ranges in this profile describe reported/traditional production conditions, **not food-safety guarantees or universal biological optima**. Strain composition, milk type, inoculum, vessel, oxygen exposure and local climate can change fermentation kinetics.
