# Matsoni / Matzoon

**Category:** traditional Caucasian fermented milk; commonly thermophilic fermentation\
**Traditional region:** Georgia and Armenia; transnational regional variation\
**Starter ecology:** diverse backslopped communities or selected autochthonous starters\
**Evidence status:** literature-backed profile; includes a later genomic correction to a published starter strain

## Origin, history and identity

Matsoni and Matzoon refer to closely related Caucasian fermented-milk traditions. A survey across Georgia and Armenia demonstrates that the category crosses national boundaries and contains substantial microbial variation. A single invention date or exclusive historical birthplace is not established by this evidence. [MAT001]

The regional name does not identify a single culture accession. Contemporary household milk, selected Georgian isolates and an industrial starter blend should have separate records. In particular, [Caspian Sea Yogurt](caspian-sea-yogurt.md), as studied and sold in Japan, must not be treated as a microbiological synonym for all Matsoni.

## Fermentation window

| Context | Temperature | Time / endpoint | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional production described in the transnational survey | **35–42 °C** | **Overnight**; the sampling description says typically **24 h** | Previously heated milk; approximately **1%** previous-batch inoculum. Different descriptions within the same paper, not a universal recipe. [MAT001] |
| Selected-culture pilot production | **44 °C** | **3 h**, followed by cooling at **6 °C overnight** | Pasteurized cow milk, **2% total starter**; study-specific manufacturing trial. [MAT004] |
| Isolate identification assays | Growth tested at **45 °C** | Not a finished-product fermentation duration | Thermophilic growth evidence; must not be substituted for a milk recipe. [MAT002] |

The catalog retains **35–42 °C** for the traditional description and records the controlled **44 °C / 3 h** trial separately here. Combining these into a single unqualified minimum-to-maximum band would hide the different starter and process contexts. “Overnight” is retained as descriptive text rather than converted into an invented numerical lower bound.

## Traditional and modern production

Traditional production transfers a small amount of fermented milk into a new heated-and-cooled batch. There are no kefir grains to recover. Such transfers maintain a community, but regional differences in the survey do not demonstrate that its composition remains constant over generations. [MAT001]

Modern development has tested **autochthonous** cultures—isolates recovered from the traditional product—to improve consistency while retaining recognizable sensory properties. Quero and colleagues selected a three-species mixture from their isolates; it combined *S. thermophilus*, *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* and *Lacticaseibacillus paracasei* (published as *Lactobacillus paracasei*). This is an experimental formulation, not a compulsory definition of Matsoni. [MAT003]

A selected bacterial blend does not automatically recreate a household fungal community. Claims of “traditional culture preservation” should specify whether they mean isolate provenance, flavour similarity, or measured community continuity.

## Milk types, sensory characteristics and texture

Cow, buffalo, goat and sheep milk were all represented in the transnational survey, and milk type was associated with community differences. This observational association does not isolate the causal effects of milk chemistry from local practices. [MAT001]

The product is an acid-set milk with a recognizable sour, milky character. In the 2021 trial, mixture B.1 was selected for a firm clot and a fresh, Matsoni-like sensory profile. Those observations are panel assessments of one formulation, not a universal quantitative texture specification. [MAT004]

For comparative records, distinguish set versus stirred samples, fat/protein content, milk heating and whey separation. None of the inspected sources supplies a single transferable viscosity value for all four milk types.

## Microbial ecology: species evidence

| Evidence and sample scope | Organisms reported | How to interpret the finding |
|---|---|---|
| Culture-based survey of **26 Georgian samples** | Predominantly *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*; *L. helveticus* also reported | Strong evidence for frequent thermophilic LAB, not a universal two-organism census. [MAT002] |
| Culture-dependent and molecular characterization | The same major yogurt organisms, plus *L. helveticus*, *Lacticaseibacillus paracasei* and *Leuconostoc lactis* | Additional LAB can be relevant to traditional character; their prevalence must remain study-specific. [MAT003] |
| **194 samples**, bacterial 16S and fungal ITS analysis | Dominant bacterial genera included *Lactobacillus* and *Streptococcus*; fungi included *Kluyveromyces marxianus*, *K. lactis*, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Candida famata* and *Lodderomyces elongisporus* | Sequence detections and regional associations; not strain-level identifications or proof of a necessary yeast function. [MAT001] |
| Genome sequencing of selected isolates | Additional verified taxa included *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus*, *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* and enterococci | A selected culture collection, not a prevalence survey of finished products. [MAT005] |

The fungal list retains names as published. No fungus is assigned as an obligatory surface-ripening organism analogous to traditional viili. Yeast detections do not justify copying a kefir yeast formula into Matsoni; neither presence nor relative DNA abundance proves viability or contribution to flavour.

## Published strain designations and identification corrections

| Published identifier | Identification and provenance | Evidence limits |
|---|---|---|
| **st265** | Reported as *S. thermophilus* in the 2021 Georgian starter trial; reidentified as **Enterococcus faecium** by whole-genome analysis in 2023 | Retain both the historical identification and the correction. It must not be listed as a genomically confirmed *S. thermophilus* starter. [MAT004], [MAT005] |
| **lbl333 / ldl333** | *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *lactis* as reported in 2021; the paper uses **lbl333** in Methods/Table 2 and **ldl333** in its conclusion | Preserve this spelling inconsistency; do not invent two independent isolates or silently claim a verified accession equivalence. [MAT004] |
| **lbr136** | *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* (published as *Lactobacillus rhamnosus*), Georgian Matsoni isolate included in mixture B.1 | A named study isolate, not the species' universal Matsoni strain. [MAT004] |
| **lbl60**, **lbl66** | Reidentified as *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* in the genomic study | Earlier *L. delbrueckii* identification was superseded. [MAT005] |
| **st237** | Reidentified as *Enterococcus faecalis*; acquired resistance and virulence findings in the same study | Detection/characterization is not a recommendation to use it as a starter. [MAT005] |

**B.1 is a mixture/batch label, not a strain ID.** Reference organisms used in laboratory assays—including *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* Lp790 and the yeast comparators—are not automatically Matsoni isolates. The paper also varies lbr105/lbr106 between Table 2 and Methods; that unresolved code is not added to the confirmed strain list. [MAT004]

## EPS, acidification and propagation behavior

The inspected product-specific evidence does not establish one canonical slime-forming organism or one chemically defined “Matsoni EPS.” Firmness or an EPS-related gene alone cannot identify a secreted polymer or quantify its contribution to the milk gel. EPS-positive isolate assays and purified-polymer studies remain a gap in this profile.

The 2021 laboratory mixture B.1 reached **pH 4.57 at 3 h** and **4.53 at 6 h**. Although the source table calls this “acidification rate,” these are **pH observations at two times**, not rate units. They apply to that selected mixture, with the identification caveat above. [MAT004]

Backslopping performance should therefore be evaluated by measured time-to-pH, viable counts and strain/community tracking. A trial using freshly prepared pure cultures does not establish an unlimited number of household transfers. Nor does species-level agreement demonstrate that the same EPS, proteolysis or acidification traits persisted.

## Storage and robustness

Quero and colleagues describe short traditional shelf life, approximately **72–120 h at 6 °C**, in their production-quality context, and report an experimental selected-culture product with shelf life extended to about **one month**. The abstract does not fully specify the longer trial's storage conditions; it is not used here as a universal one-month refrigerated shelf-life claim. [MAT003]

The 2021 pilot monitored product through day 14 under different storage temperatures; it does not license extending traditional-product storage by analogy. [MAT004] The 2023 genomic study detected prophages and distinguished problematic isolates from candidate cultures. Genomic predictions do not guarantee resistance to every phage or stability under every manufacturing stress. [MAT005]

Universal freezing, drying and recovery limits for an intact traditional community are not established. Retained fermentation activity, sensory acceptability, viable-cell survival and food safety are different outcomes and should not share a single “robust” flag.

## Distinctions and evidence quality

- **Bulgarian yogurt:** shared common LAB do not make the regional products identical; additional communities, strains, milk and process remain relevant.
- **Japanese Caspian Sea yogurt:** its FC/FA lineage cannot be substituted for the Matsoni community evidence.
- **Kefir:** Matsoni milk-to-milk propagation is distinct from grain-based culture maintenance.
- **Best-supported claims:** regional community variation; frequent thermophilic LAB; specific experimental starter performance; the genomic correction of st265.
- **Limits:** sequencing methods differ in taxonomic resolution; cultured isolates are selected subsets; traditional and pilot shelf-life values are not interchangeable. The 2023 revision demonstrates why a published strain ID and its taxonomic identification must be tracked separately.
